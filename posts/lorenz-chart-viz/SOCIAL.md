```
In my spare time, I am rebuilding our older data visualizations, the ones we built by hand back when that was the only way, using our new AI-assisted data journalism process.

For this one, it matched our hand-built numbers exactly. It also caught problems buried in the government's own data files that we could never have found by hand: a placeholder number in one year's national file, and a change in how one state counted home care workers. After a decade of doing data journalism, it's simply breathtaking to watch.

People label AI based on their experience. If your experience is typing a prompt into ChatGPT, garbage in equals garbage out. That's like saying surgery is useless when your only experience of it is playing the game "Operation." Building purpose-built agents for your specific process is not the same as what I call "everything store" AI.

What I built: the wage gap for 585 places, including every state and 393 metro areas, across about 830 occupations. It shows the share of wages that goes to the lowest-paid half of workers, how much more it takes to be in the top 10% than the median worker makes, and how the gap has changed since 2013.

One limit, up front: it only captures W-2 wages, and wages are just under half of America's personal income. The very wealthy make most of their money from capital gains, dividends and partnership profits. The new version shows how much income is missing from the chart for nearly every place.

See the wage gap where you live: https://www.data4thepeople.com/p/lorenz-chart-viz
```
