## 3. Body, part one

```
In my spare time, I am going through our older data visualizations – built by hand back when that was the only way – and rebuilding them using our new AI-assisted data journalism process. Not only am I able to add many new features that were simply impossible to build before for us, but we are able to test our process against work we tirelessly validated to see how it matches. For this one, it matched our hand-built numbers exactly. It also caught problems buried in the government's own data files that we could never have found by hand: a placeholder number in one year's national file, and a change in how one state counted home care workers.

As someone who has been doing data journalism for a decade, it's simply breathtaking to watch.

This is the thing with AI. People label it based on their experience. If your experience is recklessly typing a prompt into ChatGPT, well, garbage in equals garbage out. You will of course say AI makes mistakes, drifts, is a sycophant, because it is used in that way. But that's like saying surgery is useless when your only experience of it is playing the game "Operation." Using it properly, which means building purpose-built agents for your specific process, is not the same as what I call "everything store" AI. It can open the world to creating anything you can dream.

And so today, you can see what I dreamt up and brought to life. I created a visualization of the wage gap, illustrated by a Lorenz curve, for 585 places: the U.S., every state, 393 metro areas, every rural (nonmetropolitan) area, and even the territories. It includes about 830 occupations, sorted by average wage and immediately calculates the share of wages that goes to the lowest-paid half of paycheck workers, and how much more it takes to be in the top 10% than the median worker makes, for each place. And then it goes back in time, to 2013 for every state and 2016 for most metro areas, so you can see how the wage gap has changed.

Now, you must know before using this that it only captures W-2 wages. That is a huge limitation of this database, because wages are just under half of America's personal income. You see, the very wealthy don't make most of their money from wages. They make money from capital gains, dividends, and partnership profits. This was completely missing from the version of this visualization built by hand. With this new version, we can show you how much income is missing from the Lorenz curve for nearly every place.
```
